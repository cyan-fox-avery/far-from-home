# FAR FROM HOME

Game Concept Brief

(via ChatGPT / Mira — brought by Avery, 2026-10-07)

---

## Core concept

Far From Home is a finite incremental browser game about a child who discovers a small friendly alien and its damaged spacecraft crash-landed in their backyard.

The alien needs to get home, but its home world is unimaginably far away and the child has neither the knowledge nor the technology to repair an interstellar spacecraft. So the child makes an absurd promise anyway: they are going to figure it out.

What begins with scrap materials, library books, improvised experiments, and homemade flying contraptions slowly becomes a genuine space program. The player grows from a child tinkering in the backyard into someone capable of reaching orbit, exploring the Solar System, understanding alien technology, and eventually constructing a craft capable of interstellar travel.

The game ends when the alien successfully returns home. There is no prestige loop and no requirement to continue generating larger numbers forever. The progression is finite and story-driven, with optional postgame content after the main ending.

At its heart, Far From Home is not really about building rockets. It is about keeping a promise to a friend.

---

## Tone

The game should balance:

* wonder
* warmth
* curiosity
* gentle humour
* engineering satisfaction
* real scientific ideas
* emotional sincerity

It should never become grim or cynical.

The premise can be ridiculous without the emotions being treated as a joke. A child beginning a backyard space program is inherently funny, but the friendship between the child and alien should be genuine.

The game is not specifically a children's game, but it should be something a child could comfortably enjoy. There should be no graphic danger, cruelty, violent failure states, or emotionally punishing ending.

Failure should feel like experimentation rather than catastrophe.

The emotional target is ultimately sweet rather than bittersweet.

---

## Primary inspirations

The game has some spiritual DNA from Learn to Fly, the old browser game about a penguin repeatedly improving its equipment in order to fly farther.

The homage should not involve copying its gameplay directly. Instead, Far From Home shares its basic joy of:

build something → test it → watch it go farther than before → improve it → try again

The game will contain a direct but affectionate Easter egg through the child's stuffed penguin.

Other tonal touchstones for the alien include aspects of:

* Rocky from Project Hail Mary
* Nibbler from Futurama
* E.T.

The alien should be strange, expressive, intelligent, ridiculous, and extremely lovable rather than conventionally cute.

---

## The alien

The alien is small and friendly.

Its final design and name have not yet been decided. Its name should probably be something endearing and slightly ridiculous rather than majestic.

The alien understands its own civilization's technology in the way an ordinary modern human understands cars, phones, computers, and airplanes: it understands their purpose and basic principles but cannot independently reproduce an entire technological civilization from raw materials.

This prevents alien technology from functioning as an instant shortcut.

A useful analogy:

A modern human stranded in the Middle Ages might understand what a computer is but would still be unable to manufacture a microprocessor from scratch.

The alien therefore helps guide the player toward the necessary concepts, but the child must build the technological infrastructure required to actually reproduce or repair the relevant systems.

Over time, human and alien knowledge begin to converge.

---

## The stuffed penguin

The child owns a stuffed penguin.

The penguin becomes the program's unofficial test pilot.

Whenever a new craft needs to be tested before the alien can safely use it, Penguin gets strapped into the seat.

This is the game's main Learn to Fly Easter egg.

The penguin should gradually become a recurring visual character:

* strapped into crude early prototypes
* given goggles or a tiny homemade helmet
* acquiring mission patches
* becoming increasingly worn, patched, and decorated
* appearing in photographs and mission records
* eventually being treated almost like an actual member of the program

Possible running gag:

TEST PILOT: PENGUIN
Flight experience: extensive
Qualifications: unclear

By the end of the game, Penguin has effectively flown more missions than anyone else.

During the final launch, the alien takes Penguin along as their copilot for the journey home.

This gives the stuffed animal an emotional arc of its own: the first test pilot of the backyard space program ultimately becomes its first interstellar traveller.

## Overall story structure

The story should be designed before the mechanics. Every major gameplay system should exist because the narrative creates a reason for it.

The mechanics should serve the story rather than forcing the story to justify a predetermined upgrade tree.

### 1. The crash

The child witnesses something come down behind the house.

There is no enormous disaster. Perhaps there is a streak of light, a strange noise, and a thump from the backyard.

Outside is:

* a damaged spacecraft
* a frightened alien
* absolutely no explanation

The first stage of play revolves around very simple needs:

Help. Hide. Communicate.

The game is at its smallest here.

### 2. Communication

The child and alien initially cannot properly understand each other.

Communication develops through:

* gestures
* drawings
* objects
* symbols
* sounds
* partial use of damaged alien technology

Eventually the alien successfully communicates one concept:

HOME

Then points upward.

The child understands.

The alien is not visiting.

It is stranded.

### 3. Finding home

The first real scientific challenge is determining where the alien came from.

This introduces early astronomy.

The player may use:

* books
* star charts
* a cheap telescope
* observations
* diagrams from the alien
* fragments of navigation data recovered from the ship

Eventually the alien's star is identified.

Then comes an important emotional beat:

It is extremely far away.

This cannot be solved simply by fixing a broken engine over the weekend.

The scale of the problem becomes clear.

The child decides to try anyway.

### 4. The Backyard Space Program

The child begins learning and experimenting.

The earliest program is charmingly pathetic:

* one child
* one alien
* one stuffed penguin
* a workbench
* scrap materials
* notebooks
* household tools
* improvised test equipment

Possible humour:

BACKYARD SPACE PROGRAM

Staff: 2
Test pilots: 1 stuffed penguin
Budget: $14.63
Interstellar capability: no

This is where the primary incremental loop begins.

## Core gameplay loop

The main loop should feel like:

Learn → Build → Launch → Observe → Improve → Reach farther

The player spends time at home or at the growing space program:

* gathering or acquiring materials
* researching scientific concepts
* decoding alien systems
* improving equipment
* constructing components
* testing individual systems
* building vehicles

Then comes the reward:

The launch.

Launches should be visually satisfying enough that they feel like the payoff for preparation rather than merely another progress calculation.

---

## Launch gameplay

During a launch, the spacecraft remains roughly centred on screen while the environment moves downward around it.

The player watches the craft progressively climb through increasingly dramatic milestones.

Early examples:

Fence height

Above the house

Treetops

Higher than a bird

Above the neighbourhood

Cloud level

As technology improves:

Above the clouds

Airliner altitude

Stratosphere

Edge of space

Eventually the blue sky gradually becomes darker.

Stars begin appearing.

The Earth starts to curve below.

Then:

SPACE

The first time the player reaches space should be treated as a major achievement rather than a small notification.

---

## Later distance milestones

As the player's capabilities grow, the launch interface expands outward.

Possible progression:

Earth

* trees
* clouds
* airliner altitude
* stratosphere
* edge of space
* Kármán line
* low Earth orbit
* stable orbit

Near Earth

* high orbit
* beyond major Earth satellites
* lunar distance
* Moon

Inner Solar System

* Mars-distance capability
* asteroid belt distances
* inner-planet mission capability

Outer Solar System

* Jupiter-distance capability
* Saturn
* Uranus
* Neptune

Edge of the Solar System

* Pluto range
* Kuiper Belt
* heliopause
* interstellar space

Interstellar

Eventually:

* one light-hour
* one light-day
* increasingly enormous fractions of a light-year
* full light-year milestones
* ultimately the alien's home system

Distances should be visually compressed rather than physically proportional.

Real astronomical scale is far too large to represent literally.

The game can acknowledge this with a tiny humorous note such as:

Distances visually compressed because space is offensively large.

Planetary milestones should not imply every planet conveniently lies in a straight line. Some milestones can represent equivalent distances rather than literal flybys.

---

## Player involvement during launches

Launches should not become a complicated flight simulator.

The player should not need to understand orbital mechanics.

However, launches should involve enough interaction that they do not feel like passive cutscenes.

Possible early controls:

**Throttle**

Higher thrust means:

* faster acceleration
* greater fuel consumption
* increased heat or structural stress

**Staging**

Early rockets may require the player to manually separate stages.

Later:

AUTOMATIC STAGING COMPUTER UNLOCKED

Incremental-game progression should gradually automate tasks that once required the player's attention.

**Trajectory**

Simple directional choices rather than realistic flight controls.

For example:

STEEP ← ○ → SHALLOW

**Recovery systems**

At appropriate stages the player may:

* deploy parachutes
* activate landing systems
* choose recovery timing

Automation can eventually take over these systems as well.

---

## Failure philosophy

Nothing explodes.

This is a deliberate tonal rule.

Failures should be funny, informative, and safe enough to support the game's all-ages accessibility.

Early experiments might:

* fail to leave the ground
* wobble
* stall
* run out of thrust
* drift sideways
* land in a hedge
* become stuck in a tree
* land upside down
* fall onto a roof
* splash into a pond
* return slightly bent

Penguin remains fundamentally unharmed.

A failed mission still generates useful engineering knowledge.

The game should communicate:

It did not work. But we learned why.

This means failure is part of progress rather than punishment.

---

## The parachute milestone

At first, craft remain within heights where recovery is relatively simple.

Eventually the player develops enough propulsion that altitude stops being the only problem.

Now the question becomes:

How do we bring it back safely?

This unlocks one of the game's first major engineering milestones:

PARACHUTE

The first successful parachute test should be a memorable event.

The craft reaches its peak.

The engine stops.

It begins falling.

The player deploys the parachute.

The canopy opens.

Everything slows.

Penguin drifts safely back toward Earth.

This is important not only mechanically but thematically.

The game stops asking only:

Can we go up?

It begins asking:

Can we go somewhere and come home again?

That question echoes the entire story.

---

## Two intertwined technology paths

Far From Home should gradually develop two major technological traditions.

Human technology

Examples:

* basic electronics
* radio
* aerodynamics
* materials science
* propulsion
* rocketry
* computing
* guidance
* orbital mechanics
* satellites
* advanced manufacturing
* astronomy
* deep-space communication
* radiation shielding
* life support
* advanced propulsion

Alien technology

Initially almost incomprehensible.

Possible areas:

* alien power systems
* exotic materials
* advanced navigation
* shielding
* communication systems
* interstellar propulsion
* unknown physics

The player cannot simply unlock these by spending generic points.

Human scientific progress allows the player to gradually understand previously inaccessible alien systems.

Eventually the two technology trees merge.

That convergence is one of the game's major late-story milestones.

## Growing up

The project should span years.

The child does not remain ten years old while casually constructing an interstellar civilization over one summer.

The player gradually grows up alongside the project.

Environmental storytelling can communicate this without constantly announcing age changes:

* handwriting changes
* equipment becomes more professional
* the workbench evolves
* notebooks become scientific records
* the backyard shed expands
* school-level experiments become serious research
* eventually the project becomes an actual organization
* facilities grow far beyond the original backyard

The emotional through-line remains intact:

An adult scientific program is still fulfilling a promise originally made by a child.

The alien may age at a very different visible rate.

Penguin simply becomes increasingly battered.

---

## Expansion of the program

The backyard project should gradually outgrow the backyard.

Possible progression:

Backyard

→ shed/workshop

→ small test site

→ research laboratory

→ launch facility

→ orbital infrastructure

→ deep-space program

→ interstellar program

The comedy comes partly from remembering where this started.

Eventually thousands of people might be involved in the organization that grew from:

child + alien + stuffed penguin + screwdriver

The project should still retain little visual reminders of its beginnings.

---

## Story milestone: first spaceflight

The first flight to space should be one of the game's biggest early emotional payoffs.

The launch begins exactly like previous launches.

Trees.

Clouds.

Stratosphere.

But this time the blue keeps darkening.

Stars appear.

Earth curves below.

Then:

YOU MADE IT TO SPACE

The alien may become visibly emotional here for the first time.

Until this moment, the project has essentially been hope.

Now there is evidence that the promise might actually be possible.

---

## Story milestone: orbit

Reaching space is not enough.

The next challenge is learning how to remain there.

Stable orbit unlocks a new era of gameplay:

* satellites
* space-based observations
* improved communication
* orbital experiments
* navigation systems
* more accurate astronomy
* possibly reusable technology
* eventually off-world infrastructure

This is the point where the backyard experiment truly becomes a space program.

---

## Solar System progression

The player eventually begins making real missions away from Earth.

Not every launch should simply be an attempt to reach a larger distance number.

Missions may involve goals such as:

* reach a specified altitude
* return safely
* put an object into orbit
* deploy a satellite
* send a probe to the Moon
* return material from the Moon
* reach planetary distances
* test long-duration systems
* demonstrate communications reliability
* test shielding
* test navigation
* demonstrate life-support endurance

Every capability should ultimately contribute to solving the final interstellar problem.

---

## Voyager 1 Easter egg

Voyager 1 should appear during late deep-space progression.

The player detects something tiny.

Old.

Human.

Far from home.

It is Voyager 1.

This should be a quiet moment rather than a giant reward event.

Possible alien reaction:

"You were already saying hello."

The reference connects beautifully with the game's themes because Voyager carries humanity's Golden Record: a message sent outward into the unknown long before anyone knew whether someone would ever receive it.

The player should not interfere with Voyager.

Possible interaction:

* observe it
* photograph it
* send a harmless greeting
* log it in the program archive

A commemorative record could appear afterward.

Something like:

THE OLD TRAVELLER

A human spacecraft sent into the dark before we knew who might be listening.

Penguin may, naturally, appear somewhere in the mission photo.

---

## Discovering alien technology

For much of the game, parts of the alien ship remain mysterious.

The player may know:

this component is important

without understanding why or how it works.

As scientific capability advances, old pieces of the original crashed ship become newly understandable.

This allows discoveries from the very beginning of the game to remain relevant late into progression.

Eventually:

human science + recovered alien knowledge = hybrid technology

At this point, interstellar travel stops being theoretical.

The game transitions into its final engineering phase.

---

## The alien's secret side project

During the later stages of the game, the alien begins working on something separately.

They periodically request:

* wiring
* unusual materials
* processor time
* antennas
* energy components
* precision parts

The player helps.

The alien either refuses or fails to explain exactly what the project is.

It should feel like an affectionate running mystery rather than something ominous.

The player may assume it relates to ship repair.

It does not.

The alien is secretly building the player a gift.

---

## The final spacecraft

The late game focuses on assembling the final interstellar craft.

This should not feel like:

Buy Upgrade 97: +300% Speed

Instead, major systems should be completed individually.

For example:

* propulsion
* energy generation
* navigation
* radiation protection
* structural systems
* life support
* communications
* shielding
* guidance
* redundancy
* supplies
* final testing

The whole game has been preparing the player to understand why these systems matter.

The final craft should visually incorporate both:

* recognizable human engineering
* rebuilt alien technology

It is something neither civilization could have built alone under these circumstances.

---

## Final test

Before departure, the player may perform one last major unmanned or Penguin-crewed test.

The launch begins.

Milestones that once represented enormous achievements now fly past rapidly:

Trees.

Clouds.

Space.

Orbit.

Moon distance.

Mars distance.

Jupiter.

Neptune.

Kuiper Belt.

Voyager distance.

Heliopause.

The player finally sees how dramatically far the program has come.

What once took the entire game to achieve is now merely the opening stretch.

---

## Launch day

Eventually everything is ready.

The alien is going home.

The goodbye should be emotional but restrained.

This is not designed as a tragedy.

The child, now likely grown, hands Penguin to the alien.

Penguin takes the copilot seat.

The oldest test pilot in the program gets the final mission.

Then:

LAUNCH HOME

The final launch uses the same visual language as every launch before it.

That is important.

The player watches the craft pass:

the trees
the clouds
space
orbit
the Moon
planetary distances
the outer Solar System
Voyager
the heliopause
interstellar space

All the milestones that once seemed impossible.

Eventually the alien's home marker, which has existed impossibly far away for much of the game, begins getting closer.

---

## Home

The alien successfully reaches home.

This should be explicit.

No ambiguity.

No silent ending suggesting that perhaps something went wrong.

The player succeeded.

The promise was kept.

---

## The interstellar walkie-talkie

After the departure, the player returns to the now strangely quiet program.

The alien's mysterious side project is revealed.

It is a communication device.

Essentially:

AN INTERSTELLAR WALKIE-TALKIE

The alien spent years secretly solving the opposite half of the problem.

While the player was building a way for the alien to go home, the alien was building a way for them not to lose each other once they did.

This may require one deliberate fictional leap beyond known physics. That is acceptable.

The science throughout the game can remain grounded, but the walkie-talkie gets permission to violate relativity because:

friendship.

The device activates.

Static.

A signal.

Then:

Hello?

The alien is home.

Penguin is there too.

The distance remains.

The friendship does not end.

This converts the finale from bittersweet into overwhelmingly sweet.

---

## Postgame

The main story is complete after the alien reaches home.

There is no endless prestige loop.

However, the completed game can remain explorable.

Possible postgame activities:

* revisit old missions
* inspect the program's history
* view scientific discoveries
* see photographs
* browse Penguin's missions
* examine recovered or archived equipment
* continue limited optional research
* receive a finite collection of messages from the alien's world

Possible transmissions:

* confirmation that they arrived safely
* meeting alien family members
* alien jokes that translate terribly
* pictures of Penguin on another planet
* scientific discoveries being exchanged
* everyday mundane messages

The final-final message does not need a giant speech.

Something as simple as:

Same time tomorrow?

would be enough.

## Title screen

The working/favoured title is:

FAR FROM HOME

The title-screen image is already conceptually established.

A silhouette of the child and alien together in the backyard at sunset.

They are looking upward.

Possibly sitting or standing in the grass.

Elements may include:

* warm orange/pink horizon
* sky fading into deep blue
* first stars appearing
* shed or workshop silhouette
* faint outline of the damaged spacecraft
* Penguin beside them or under the child's arm
* subtle backyard details

The image should communicate the entire emotional premise:

They are tiny. The universe is enormous. They are going to try anyway.

The title screen may subtly evolve as the game progresses, showing increasingly advanced infrastructure appearing beyond the same backyard.

## Scientific approach

Far From Home should use real science whenever doing so makes the game more interesting.

It does not need to be a strict simulator.

Scientific ideas should influence:

* progression
* mission design
* terminology
* technology
* environmental storytelling

Good opportunities include:

* aerodynamics
* thrust
* fuel
* staging
* orbital mechanics
* radio communication
* astronomical distances
* spectroscopy
* material properties
* radiation
* planetary environments
* life support
* propulsion
* observational astronomy

The game should not drown the player in formulas.

Educational details should emerge naturally from play.

Late-game alien technology permits speculative science.

The interstellar walkie-talkie is intentionally allowed to be softer science than the rest.

## Important design principles

1. Story first

The story dictates the systems.

Never add a mechanic merely because incremental games are expected to have it.

2. Finite progression

There is a real ending.

The player is building toward something specific.

3. No prestige resets

The player does not throw away their progress to multiply future production.

That would undermine the narrative.

4. Progress should be visible

The player should physically see themselves getting farther.

5. Small beginnings matter

The late-game space agency must never completely erase the memory of the backyard.

6. Failure teaches

Failed tests create information rather than punishment.

7. No explosions

The tone remains safe, warm, and accessible.

8. Automation represents mastery

Things the player once did manually become automated when the program learns how to do them reliably.

9. Science supports wonder

Real concepts should make the universe feel larger and stranger, not turn the game into homework.

10. The relationship is the real progression

Technology changes dramatically.

The core promise does not.

## Technical scope

Far From Home is intended as a browser game and should be compatible with static hosting such as GitHub Pages.

Likely implementation:

* HTML
* CSS
* JavaScript
* local save system using browser storage
* structured data files for technologies, missions, dialogue, milestones, etc.

No backend should be necessary for the core game.

The game does not currently require:

* multiplayer
* user accounts
* global leaderboards
* cloud saves
* server-authoritative logic

This makes GitHub Pages a realistic deployment target.

## The emotional thesis

The alien falls out of the sky.

A child promises to help them get home.

Neither of them knows how.

So they learn.

They build.

They fail safely.

They try again.

The backyard becomes a workshop.

The workshop becomes a laboratory.

The laboratory becomes a space program.

The child grows up.

Penguin keeps flying.

Human knowledge and alien knowledge gradually meet in the middle.

Eventually the impossible distance becomes crossable.

The alien goes home.

Penguin goes with them.

And when the player thinks the story is finally over, a strange little radio lights up.

Because the player spent years building their friend a way home.

Their friend spent years making sure that home would never mean goodbye.
